Working With Human Nature
Agile Doesn't Fix Humans. It Accounts for Them.
The system is designed around how we actually think, estimate, and behave.
Relative Sizing
We're better at "bigger than" than "how long"
Humans are terrible at estimating in hours — but we're naturally good at saying "this is bigger than that." Agile uses that ability instead of fighting it. We compare work to other work rather than guessing at time.
Humphreys Law
People don't know what they want until they see what they don't
Requirements written in a conference room are hypotheses. Demos turn hypotheses into real feedback. This is why agile shows the work early and often — not at the end when it's too late to change course.
Cone of Uncertainty
Precision at the start of a project is an illusion
At project start, estimates can vary 4x in either direction. Agile respects this. Short iterations replace guesswork with real information — rather than defending a number that was never reliable.
Procrastination
Humans spike toward deadlines — so shorten the loop
Everyone waits until the last minute — it's human nature. On a six-month project, that panic arrives late and hits hard. On a two-week sprint, the same behavior happens but the damage is small and contained. Four small crunches instead of one catastrophic one.
Human Behavior · Procrastination Spikes
Agile Doesn't Stop the Spike. It Shrinks It.
Humans naturally defer. The question is how much damage occurs when they do.
Waterfall / Long Timebox
Work drifts flat. Then panic. One catastrophic crunch at the end — too late to recover or incorporate feedback.
Agile / 2-Week Sprints
Same human behavior. Compressed into two weeks. Damage is contained. Learning happens at every spike — not just the last one.
Zoom In — The 2-Week Sprint
Week 1 is learning. Week 2 is demo and feedback. The shape is honest — slow start as the team finds footing, then acceleration toward a real feedback event. Every sprint is a complete loop.

The 4 Pillars
What Agile Actually Is
Not a tool. Not a ceremony. A shift in what we value.
👥
Individuals and interactions over processes and tools Better processes and tools don't fix a broken system. The people closest to the work — talking to each other, solving problems together — do. Agile puts them first.
🚢
Working deliverables over comprehensive documentation People only know what they want when they see what they don't. The only way to find that out is to put something real in front of them. A working deliverable creates that conversation. A document never will.
🤝
Customer collaboration over contract negotiation Someone hands us a requirement and disappears. Six months later it's wrong. Agile replaces that handoff with a continuous conversation — so we build what's actually needed, not what was assumed.
🔄
Responding to change over following a plan The plan you make on day one will be wrong. That's not a failure — it's reality. What matters is building a team that can adapt when things change, not one that's locked into defending a document.
Dave Snowden & Mary Boone · Harvard Business Review · 2007
You're Using the Wrong Tool
Most IT knowledge work lives in the Complex domain. We keep applying Complicated tools to it.
Complex
Probe → Sense → Respond
You can't know the answer upfront. Try something small, see what happens, and adjust. This is how good IT work actually gets done. Agile, Scrum, Kanban belong here.
Complicated
Sense → Analyze → Respond
Waterfall assumes you already know the answer. Plan everything upfront, execute in order, deliver at the end. That works here. The problem is — most IT work doesn't actually live here.
Chaotic
Act → Sense → Respond
The building is on fire. You don't hold a meeting. Act first, stabilize the situation, then figure out what happened and why.
Simple
Sense → Categorize → Respond
You've done this a hundred times. The answer is known. Follow the established process and move on.

Flow Delivery Experiment
This Is Not an "Agile Rollout."
It's a two-week experiment. One team. Observable outcomes. Executive belief is not required — observable outcomes are.
Pre-work
Align Before You Begin
Role alignment, intake rules, baseline snapshot. Define what success looks like before the first sprint starts. Name the Flow Coordinator. Name the PO. Agree on pivot conditions.
Week One
Start the Shift
Board live. Interrupts logged. Daily flow sync. PO makes tradeoffs explicit. By end of week: interrupts are visible and counted, blocked work is explicit, tradeoffs are verbalized — not implied.
Week Two
Stabilize and Decide
Reinforce WIP limits, tighten intake, run retro. Decision gate: Continue if outcomes improve. Adjust if mechanics need tuning. Pivot if visibility can't be enforced.
Guardrails
What We Will Not Do
This experiment has teeth. It also has limits. Both matter.
✕
No "We're Agile Now" DeclarationThis isn't a brand. It's a behavior change. Declarations create compliance theater.
✕
No Ceremony Compliance as a Permanent StateWhen you're learning Agile for the first time, going through the motions is fine — that's how habits form. But ceremonies that never produce a decision or a course correction become theater. The goal is understanding why each one exists.
✕
No Hiding KTLO or InterruptionsMaking reactive work invisible to protect optics is exactly what we're fighting. Sunshine laws apply to everything.
✕
No Performance Scoring via Agile MetricsVelocity is not a productivity score. Story points are a planning tool. Measuring people with team metrics is a misuse of the data.

Agile is not the outcome. Disciplined, predictable delivery is the outcome.

A Note to the Skeptics
Three Voices That Block Change
They are real. They are understandable. And they are worth naming out loud.
Voice of Judgement
Shuts down an open mind. Prevents us from seeing possibilities and learning from others. Shows up as: "We tried this before." "This won't work here." "That's not how we do things."
Voice of Cynicism
Blocks empathy and compassion. Makes it difficult to connect with others and their ideas. Shows up as: "Leadership just wants a buzzword." "Nothing ever really changes." "Who came up with this?"
Voice of Fear
Inhibits willingness to take risks. Prevents stepping into the unknown to create meaningful change. Shows up as: "What if I fail?" "What does this mean for my role?" "I don't want to be exposed."
The ask is not certainty. The ask is two weeks of honest effort and open eyes.
Lived Experience · Our Team · Two Years In
What Actually Changed
Not theory. This is what happened when we worked differently.
Capacity
We discovered 52% of our work was reactive and unplanned. We couldn't commit accurately because we couldn't see what was coming. Making it visible changed everything about how we planned.
Context Switching
Agile didn't eliminate the chaos — it made the tradeoffs impossible to ignore. When an urgent request arrives mid-sprint, the team and leaders now have to say out loud what gets dropped to make room. That uncomfortable conversation used to happen in silence. Now it happens in the open.
Dependencies
Cross-team dependencies were our biggest source of delay. Early dependency mapping during refinement surfaced blockers before they became emergencies. Lead time dropped.
Team Health
The team is happier. They show up excited. They deliver consistently — not because we demanded more, but because we removed the friction that was exhausting them. The system changed. The people responded.
